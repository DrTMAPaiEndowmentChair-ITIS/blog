In August 2026 OpenAI published a collection of ten results in mathematics and theoretical computer science, each with a Lean certificate.[^1] Three of the chapters sit in the same corner of the subject: the geometry of numbers, where the objects are lattices, packings, codes, and convex bodies, and the technique of choice is a linear program over Fourier-positive test functions.

Chapters 1, 2, and 8 improve three bounds that had stood for a long time. Two of them dated from 1977 and 1978. The third was a conjecture of Ehrhart from 1964. This post explains what each problem asks, what was previously known, and the shape of the arguments.

## Sphere packing in high dimensions

Let $\Delta_d$ be the largest density achievable by a packing of congruent balls in $\mathbb R^d$. Lower bounds are easy and weak: random constructions give roughly $2^{-d}$. Upper bounds are hard, and for fifty years the best general one came from Kabatianskii and Levenshtein, who proved $\Delta_d \le 2^{-(0.59905576\ldots + o(1))d}$.

The dominant tool for upper bounds is a linear program introduced by Gorbachev and, independently, Cohn and Elkies.[^2] Fix the Fourier convention $\hat f(\xi)=\int f(x)e^{-2\pi i x\cdot\xi}\,dx$ and let $v_d$ be the volume of the unit ball. Define the admissible class

$$
\mathcal A_d=\bigl\{f\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R^d;\mathbb R)\;:\;\hat f(0)>0,\ \hat f\ge 0 \text{ on } \mathbb R^d,\ f\le 0 \text{ on } \{|x|\ge 1\}\bigr\}
$$

and the resulting bound

$$
\mathrm{LP}_d=\frac{v_d}{2^d}\inf_{f\in\mathcal A_d}\frac{f(0)}{\hat f(0)},
\qquad \Delta_d\le \mathrm{LP}_d .
$$

Every admissible $f$ is a certificate. Poisson summation turns the two sign conditions into an inequality about any packing at all: the spatial condition kills contributions from distinct centers, and the Fourier condition makes the remaining sum nonnegative. This is the framework in which Viazovska settled dimension 8 and, with collaborators, dimension 24, by producing exactly optimal "magic" functions.

<PackingLPDuality />

What nobody knew was how strong the program is in general. Cohn and Zhao showed it is at least as strong as the Kabatianskii-Levenshtein bound.[^3] Whether it is strictly stronger in the exponent was open, though Afkhami-Jeddi, Cohn, Hartman, de Laat, and Tajdini conjectured the answer while studying the modular bootstrap.

Chapter 1 proves that conjecture.

$$
\limsup_{d\to\infty}\Delta_d^{1/d}\ \le\ \lim_{d\to\infty}\mathrm{LP}_d^{1/d}=\sqrt{\frac{e}{2\pi}}
$$

Written as an exponent, $\Delta_d\le 2^{-(\alpha_*+o(1))d}$ with $\alpha_*=\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi/e)=0.6044\ldots$, against the classical $0.59905576\ldots$. Intervening work had improved only lower-order factors, so this is the first change to the exponent since 1978.

<PackingExponentGain />

The result is two-sided, and the second half matters as much as the first. The limit is an equality, so no Cohn-Elkies auxiliary function can ever do better. The linear program is now a closed question at the level of exponents, and further progress on sphere packing must come from somewhere else.

## Sign uncertainty as the underlying obstruction

The lower bound on $\mathrm{LP}_d$ runs through an uncertainty principle. For $g\in L^1(\mathbb R^d;\mathbb R)$ with $\hat g=\varsigma g$, $\varsigma\in\{-1,+1\}$, let $r(g)$ be the smallest $R$ beyond which $g\ge 0$, and set

$$
A_\varsigma(d)=\inf\bigl\{r(g)\;:\;0\neq g\in L^1(\mathbb R^d;\mathbb R),\ \hat g=\varsigma g,\ g(0)=0\bigr\}.
$$

A self-dual function that vanishes at the origin cannot become nonnegative too soon. The $+1$ case is the Bourgain-Clozel-Kahane problem; the $-1$ case was introduced by Cohn and Gonçalves and tied to sphere packing.[^4] Chapter 1 settles both asymptotically.

$$
\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{A_+(d)}{\sqrt d}=\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{A_-(d)}{\sqrt d}=\frac1\pi
$$

The link to packing is a two-line construction. Given admissible $F$, rescale by $a=(\hat F(0)/F(0))^{1/d}$ so that $h(x)=F(ax)$ satisfies $h(0)=\hat h(0)$. Then $g=\hat h-h$ is anti-self-Fourier, vanishes at the origin, and is nonnegative outside the ball of radius $1/a$. Since $\int g=0$, half of its total mass is negative, and all of that negative mass sits inside that ball. A Mellin-transform estimate shows a radial eigenfunction with $g(0)=0$ has exponentially little $L^1$ mass inside radius $c\sqrt d$ for any $c<1/\pi$, which forces $1/a\ge(1/\pi-o(1))\sqrt d$. The matching upper bound modifies the Mellin transform of a Gaussian to build a Fourier pair whose sign conditions begin at $(1/\pi+o(1))\sqrt d$. Stirling's formula converts radii into densities.

The two constants are asymptotically equal but not equal: the paper records $A_+(d)<A_-(d)$ in every dimension.

## Binary and spherical codes

Chapter 2 attacks the discrete analogues and arrives at the same exponent by a different road.

A binary code $C\subseteq\{0,1\}^n$ with minimum Hamming distance $d$ has size at most $A_2(n,d)$; a spherical code $C\subseteq S^{n-1}$ with pairwise inner products at most $s$ has size at most $A(n,s)$. The asymptotic rates are

$$
R_2(\delta)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log_2 A_2(n,\lceil\delta n\rceil),
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{sph}}(s)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log_2 A(n,s).
$$

The standing records were the second McEliece-Rodemich-Rumsey-Welch bound $M_2(\delta)$ from 1977 and the Kabatianskii-Levenshtein bound $B_{\mathrm{KL}}(s)$ from 1978. Both come from Delsarte's two-point linear program: a polynomial $F=\sum_j f_j P_j$ in the appropriate orthogonal basis, with $f_0>0$, $f_j\ge 0$, and $F\le 0$ at every permitted inner product, certifies $|C|\le F(1)/f_0$.

The new idea is to change what sits at each code point. In the classical construction each retained harmonic space contributes one vector per code point. Here a $d_E$-dimensional subspace is attached to each point $x$ inside a common $D$-dimensional ambient space, moving with the point under the symmetry group, so that the overlap $\operatorname{tr}(P_xP_y)$ remains a scalar function of distance. Writing $\Lambda$ for the largest eigenvalue of the associated weighted transition matrix, the projection bound reads

$$
|C|\le \frac{1-s}{\Lambda-s}\cdot\frac{D}{d_E}\qquad(\Lambda>s).
$$

An exponentially large projection rank $d_E$ divides straight into the bound, provided $\Lambda$ stays clear of the threshold. The transition matrices come from representation graphs: tridiagonal in the binary case, and in the spherical case weighted graphs whose vertices are indexed by Young diagrams, with $r$ rows for the subspace at each code point and one more row for the ambient representation. That hierarchy level $r$ is the new parameter.

For binary codes the improvement is stated as a strict inequality against the fully optimized classical exponent, using an explicit variational quantity $\kappa_{\mathrm{bin}}$:

$$
R_2(\delta)\le \kappa_{\mathrm{bin}}(\delta)<M_2(\delta)\qquad\text{for every fixed } 0<\delta<1/2.
$$

The classical bounds are boundary cases of the enlarged problem, obtained by setting the attached harmonic degrees to zero. For spherical codes the hierarchy is strictly monotone at every level:

$$
R_{\mathrm{sph}}(s)\le\inf_{r\ge0}\overline\kappa_r(s)<\overline\kappa_1(s)<\overline\kappa_{\mathrm{row}}(s)<\overline\kappa_0(s)=B_{\mathrm{KL}}(s).
$$

Level $r=0$ is exactly the classical construction. Every subsequent level strictly improves it, for every fixed $s\in(0,1)$.

Because a packing bound follows from a spherical-code bound by hemisphere projection, the hierarchy also produces

$$
\Delta_n\le 2^{-(\lambda_*+o(1))n},\qquad \lambda_*=\tfrac12\log_2\frac{2\pi}{e},
$$

which is the Chapter 1 exponent, recovered as the small-angle limit $s\uparrow1$. Two independent methods, one Euclidean and Fourier-analytic, one spherical and representation-theoretic, meeting at the same constant is the strongest available evidence that the constant is the right one.

## Ehrhart's volume conjecture

Chapter 8 is a different kind of problem with the same flavour: a convexity bound forced by a lattice condition.

Ehrhart asked in 1964 how large a convex body can be if its barycenter is its only interior lattice point. The natural candidate is the dilated, shifted simplex $(n+1)\Delta_n-(1,\ldots,1)$, and the conjecture is that it is extremal.

$$
K\subset\mathbb R^n \text{ compact convex, barycenter } 0,\ \operatorname{int}(K)\cap\mathbb Z^n=\{0\}
\ \Longrightarrow\
\operatorname{vol}(K)\le\frac{(n+1)^n}{n!}
$$

<PackingEhrhartSimplex />

Ehrhart proved it in the plane and for simplices in every dimension. For general centered bodies, Milman-Pajor symmetrization with Minkowski's theorem gives $\operatorname{vol}(K)\le 4^n$, and a sequence of refinements shaved subexponential and then exponential factors off that, the last of them leaning on Klartag and Lehec's resolution of Bourgain's slicing problem.[^5] Since $(n+1)^n/n!$ grows like $e^n$ up to polynomial factors, all of those bounds were exponentially far from sharp.

The proof is complex-analytic. A theorem of Berman and Berndtsson supplies a smooth convex potential $\varphi$ whose gradient transports $e^{-\varphi(x)}dx$ to Lebesgue measure on $K$.[^6] Pulled back to $X=(\mathbb C^*)^n$ through logarithmic radii, the weighted holomorphic space $\mathcal H_k$ has Laurent monomial basis indexed by $\mathbb Z^n\cap\operatorname{int}(kK)$, so the hypothesis that $0$ is the only interior lattice point says precisely that $\mathcal H_1=\mathbb C$.

That is the pivot. Filtering an orthonormal basis by vanishing order at $p=(1,\ldots,1)$ produces a ray of potentials $(\psi_t)_{t\ge0}$ with $\psi_0=\varphi$, and one studies

$$
Z(t)=\frac1{\operatorname{vol}(K)}\int_X e^{-\psi_t}\,d\nu,\qquad L(t)=-\log Z(t).
$$

Berndtsson's positivity theorem makes $L$ convex, because $\mathcal H_1=\mathbb C$ keeps the relevant Bergman kernel equal to $1/(\operatorname{vol}(K)Z(t))$. Counting how many linear conditions vanishing to a given order imposes bounds the initial slope from below; a local Schwarz estimate on a ball of radius proportional to $e^{-t/2}$ bounds it from above. With $c_K=(n!\operatorname{vol}(K))^{1/n}$,

$$
\frac{n}{n+1}\,c_K\ \le\ L'(0^+)\ \le\ n,
$$

so $c_K\le n+1$, which is the theorem. The equality refinement, that every extremizer is a unimodular image of the centered simplex, is not settled here.

## What the cluster has in common

All three arguments convert a combinatorial or geometric constraint into a positivity condition on an analytic object, then extract a sharp constant from the exact asymptotics of that object. In Chapter 1 the object is a Fourier eigenfunction and the constant falls out of Mellin analysis of a modified Gaussian. In Chapter 2 it is a family of projections indexed by Young diagrams and the constant is the top eigenvalue of a weighted graph. In Chapter 8 it is a Bergman kernel and the constant is a slope pinned between a dimension count and a Schwarz estimate.

Two of the three come with matching lower bounds, which is the more consequential structural fact. The Cohn-Elkies program cannot be pushed further, and the spherical hierarchy converges to the same wall. Anyone hoping to move the sphere-packing exponent again now knows which tools are exhausted.
